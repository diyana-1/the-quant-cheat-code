In Plain English

The 3 Real-World Ingredients of Any Market Move

Everyday Market News Analogy

Forget abstract jargon like "stochastic perturbations" or "white noise". Every single model in Hull Chapter 14 simply combines three everyday real-world questions:

1. The Expected Trend ($a \, dt$) 📈

Where is it naturally heading?

The predictable baseline growth over time (like steady interest in a savings account or a company's average annual growth). If zero unexpected news broke today, the price would simply glide along this steady trend.

2. Breaking News Shock ($dz$) ⚡

What unexpected news just hit?

Surprise real-world events that hit the wire right now (e.g. an unexpected earnings surprise or sudden rate cut). It is a fair random shock with zero expectation: half the news is good ($+dz$), half is bad ($-dz$).

3. Shock Sensitivity ($b$) 💥

How hard does the price react?

How violently the price jumps in response to the news shock. A sleepy utility stock barely moves when news breaks ($b$ is small); an unpredictable tech stock jumps dramatically ($b$ is large).

The Generalized Wiener Process

John Hull, Chapter 14 (Sections 14.1 & 14.2): Moving from pure random Brownian motion ($dz$) to processes with steady drift ($a$) and market volatility ($b$).

Core Milestone Card

The Generalized Wiener Process ($dx = a\,dt + b\,dz$)

John Hull Section 14.2
1. Formal Definition SDE Form

Constant Drift & Variance Rate

$$dx = a \, dt + b \, dz$$

A continuous stochastic process where variable $x$ changes with a constant expected drift rate $a$ and a constant variance rate $b^2$. Over any time interval $T$, the accumulated change $\Delta x$ is normally distributed:

$$\Delta x \sim \mathcal{N}(aT, \; b^2 T)$$

Where $dz = \epsilon \sqrt{dt}$ is a standard Wiener increment with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, $\mathbb{E}[\Delta z] = 0$, and $\text{Var}(\Delta z) = \Delta t$.

2. In Plain English Core Intuition

A Steady Highway with Sudden Road Bumps

Standard Brownian motion floats aimlessly like dust in water with zero expected direction ($a = 0$). The Generalized Wiener Process gives that particle an engine:

🏎️ $a \, dt$ — Cruise-Control Trend

The predictable forward speed. Over 1 full year ($T = 1$), your balance steadily advances by $a$.

📰 $dz$ — Breaking News Shocks

Today's unexpected market surprises. Purely random, zero-centered shocks ($\mathbb{E}[\Delta z] = 0$).

📊 $b$ — Shock Sensitivity Scale

The shock volume dial. A larger $b$ means far wilder swings whenever news hits.

💡 Gaussian Result: Because every tiny shock is drawn from a normal distribution, adding thousands of them creates a smooth Gaussian bell curve.
3. Core Exam Takeaway Hull Exam Traps

Overdrafts & The $b$ vs $b^2$ Distinction

⚠️ Trap 1: $b$ is NOT the variance rate!

In Hull's questions, if variance rate is $900M, then $b^2 = 900 \implies b = \sqrt{900} = 30$. Never multiply $900 \times 1.96$!

🎯 Formula: 95% Confidence Bounds

$$(x_0 + aT) \pm 1.96 \, b\sqrt{T}$$

💡 Why it fails for stocks:

In Hull Example 14.1, cash bounds are [-$38.8M, +$78.8M]. Cash accounts can drop into negative overdraft loans, but stock prices cannot breach zero ($S \gt 0$) due to limited liability!

Foundational Warm-Up: The Standard Wiener Process ($dz$)

Hull Section 14.1 Foundation
1. Raw Increment: $dz = \epsilon\sqrt{dt}$

The basic building block of stochastic calculus. The shock is scaled by $\sqrt{dt}$, driven by standard normal draw $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

2. Mean & Variance Rules

$\mathbb{E}[\Delta z] = 0$ (zero drift / no bias) and $\text{Var}(\Delta z) = \Delta t$ (variance grows strictly linearly with time, so std dev is $\sqrt{\Delta t}$).

3. Independent Increments (Markov)

Changes across non-overlapping intervals are independent. Setting $a = 0, b = 1$ in the Generalized Wiener process recovers this exact pure Wiener motion.

Mathematical Anatomy & Formula Mechanics

Connecting continuous stochastic calculus to discrete computer simulations.

Formula Breakdown
Continuous Differential Equation
$$dx = a \, dt + b \, dz$$

The Expected Trend: $a \, dt$

$a$ is the drift rate (expected trend per unit of time). Over a time horizon $T$, the expected total change in $x$ is simply $a T$. If there were zero unexpected shocks ($b = 0$), $x(t) = x_0 + a t$ (a predictable straight line).

Market Shock Response: $b \, dz$

⚠️ Exam Trap: $b$ is NOT variance!

In $b \, dz$, $dz$ is the raw random news shock (today's unpredictable market surprise), while $b$ is the volatility / shock sensitivity (standard deviation rate) that decides how violently the asset swings. The variance rate is $b^2$, not $b$! (For example, if $b = 3$, the variance rate per year is $3^2 = 9$).

🔗 Continuous Calculus → Discrete Simulation Step
Coding Formula
$$\Delta x = a \, \Delta t + b \, \epsilon \sqrt{\Delta t}, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$
Step 1: Continuous SDE
Differential $dx = a \, dt + b \, dz$

Continuous calculus describes infinitesimally tiny slices $dt$.

Step 2: Discretization
Scale by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ & Draw $\epsilon$

Replace $dz$ with $\epsilon \sqrt{\Delta t}$ because variance scales linearly with time: $\text{Var}(\Delta z) = \Delta t$.

🔗 Expected Mean & Variance → 95% Confidence Interval
Exam Formula
$$x(T) \sim \mathcal{N}\left(x_0 + a T, \; b^2 T\right) \implies 95\% \text{ Interval}: \left(x_0 + a T\right) \pm 1.96 \, b \sqrt{T}$$
Step 1: Distribution
Gaussian Additivity

The sum of independent normal increments is also normal with mean $a T$ and variance $b^2 T$.

Step 2: The $1.96$ Normal Multiplier
95% Margin: $\pm 1.96 \times \text{Standard Deviation}$

In a normal bell curve, exactly 95% of all outcomes fall within $\pm 1.96$ standard deviations of the mean ($z_{0.025} = 1.96$). Since the standard deviation is $\sqrt{b^2 T} = b\sqrt{T}$, the 95% spread is $1.96 \times b\sqrt{T}$.

Demystifying John Hull's Exam Terminology

💵 1. What does "M" mean?

In John Hull's textbook, $M$ means Million Dollars ($1M = $1,000,000). The company starts with a cash balance in millions, so an expected drift of $20M means cash is growing by 20 million dollars per year.

🔔 2. Is this the Normal Distribution?

Yes, exactly! Since every tiny shock $dz$ is drawn from a normal distribution, adding them up over time $T$ creates a perfect Gaussian bell curve: $\Delta x \sim \mathcal{N}(aT, \; b^2 T)$. That is why we use the standard normal critical value $z = 1.96$ for the 95% confidence interval!

⚠️ 3. Why can cash turn negative?

Because a normal distribution extends to $-\infty$, the lower bound is -$38.8M. Bank accounts can enter negative balances (an overdraft loan). However, stock prices cannot go below zero, which is why stocks use Geometric Brownian Motion (Milestone 3) instead!

The Itô Process: Making Drift & Volatility Adaptive

John Hull, Chapter 14 (Section 14.3): Why fixed constants fail in financial markets and how state-dependent drift $a(x,t)$ and volatility $b(x,t)$ unlock stock price modeling.

Core Milestone Card

The Itô Process ($dx = a(x,t)\,dt + b(x,t)\,dz$)

John Hull Section 14.3
1. Formal Definition SDE Form

Adaptive Drift & Variance Rates

$$dx = a(x, t) \, dt + b(x, t) \, dz$$

An Itô Process is a generalized stochastic differential equation where both the expected drift rate $a(x, t)$ and the variance rate $b(x, t)^2$ are functions of the underlying variable $x$ and time $t$.

$$dz = \epsilon \sqrt{dt}, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Unlike constant-coefficient Wiener processes, the parameters dynamically evolve as the state variable $x$ changes and the clock ticks forward.

2. In Plain English Core Intuition

Why Fixed Dollar Steps Fail in Financial Markets

A Generalized Wiener process forces constant dollar moves. But a $10 penny stock and a $600,000 Berkshire share cannot possibly fluctuate by the same dollar sum!

📈 Drift Scales with Price: $a(S, t) = \mu S$

A 10% expected return yields +$10 gain on a $100 stock, but +$20 on a $200 stock. Drift grows in proportion to asset size.

⚡ Volatility Scales with Price: $b(S, t) = \sigma S$

Swings shrink naturally to fractions of a cent as price nears zero, preventing stock prices from crashing through the zero floor.

💡 Return Stabilization: Dividing both sides by $S$ gives stationary percentage returns: $\frac{\Delta S}{S} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu \Delta t, \sigma^2 \Delta t)$.
3. Core Exam Takeaway Hull Exam Focus

The Foundation of Geometric Brownian Motion

🎯 John Hull's Exam Bridge:

In Section 14.3, John Hull sets $a(S,t) = \mu S$ and $b(S,t) = \sigma S$. This transforms the generic Itô process directly into Geometric Brownian Motion: $$dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dz$$

🛡️ Natural Zero Floor ($S \gt 0$):

Because volatility scales with $S$, as $S \to 0$, dollar volatility $\sigma S \to 0$. Stock prices naturally cannot cross zero into negative territory!

Optional Preview: Looking Ahead to Itô's Lemma

Advanced / Optional

You don't need this to understand the Itô process! This is just a sneak peek for options pricing.

Hull Section 14.5 Preview

While an Itô Process models how the underlying asset $S$ moves, Itô's Lemma is the mathematical tool used to find the process followed by a derivative or option $G(S, t)$ whose value depends on $S$:

Itô's Lemma Formula for $G(x, t)$
$$dG = \left( \frac{\partial G}{\partial x} a + \frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial x^2} b^2 \right) dt + \frac{\partial G}{\partial x} b \, dz$$
? Where does $(dz)^2 = dt$ come from? (The Stochastic Multiplication Rule)

In normal calculus, squaring small changes gives $(dx)^2 \approx 0$. But in stochastic calculus, squaring noise produces real time ($dt$)! Here is the exact proof:

Step 1: Shock Definition
$dz = \epsilon \sqrt{dt}$

Wiener increments are scaled by $\sqrt{dt}$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ is standard normal.

Step 2: Square Both Sides
$(dz)^2 = \epsilon^2 dt$

Squaring $\epsilon\sqrt{dt}$ yields $\epsilon^2 (\sqrt{dt})^2 = \epsilon^2 dt$.

Step 3: Average of $\epsilon^2$
$\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = 1 \implies (dz)^2 \to dt$

Because $\epsilon$ has mean 0 and variance 1, $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = 1$. As $dt \to 0$, fluctuations vanish, leaving deterministically $(dz)^2 = dt$!

$(dz)^2 = dt$ (Survives!)
•
$dt \cdot dz = 0$ (Vanishes)
•
$(dt)^2 = 0$ (Vanishes)

🛡️ Milestone 3 Deep-Dive

Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)

John Hull, Chapter 14 (Sections 14.3 & 14.4): The premier continuous-time model for equity prices that protects limited liability.

Core Milestone Card

Geometric Brownian Motion ($dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dz$)

John Hull Section 14.3 & 14.4
1. Formal Definition SDE & Closed Form

Percentage Returns & Lognormal Stock Prices

$$\frac{dS}{S} = \mu \, dt + \sigma \, dz$$

An Itô process where the drift rate $\mu S$ and volatility rate $\sigma S$ scale with price $S$. Applying Itô's Lemma to $G = \ln S$ yields the log-price process first:

$$dG = d(\ln S) = \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)dt + \sigma \, dz$$

Integrating and exponentiating gives the exact future stock price formula:

$$S(T) = S_0 \exp\left( \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}Z \right)$$

Where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, proving future stock prices are lognormally distributed with strictly positive support ($S(T) \gt 0$).

2. In Plain English Core Intuition

Percentage Gains & The Natural Zero Floor

Stockholders invest for percentage gains, not fixed dollars. A $5 drop is a trivial blip for Berkshire ($600,000) but catastrophic for a $10 stock. In GBM, all shocks scale with current price:

🏷️ Fair Percentage Swings ($\frac{dS}{S}$)

Scaling by $S$ keeps percentage returns stationary across all price regimes, perfectly matching financial market reality.

🛡️ Limited Liability Floor ($S \gt 0$)

Because Euler's exponent $e^n \gt 0$ for any real number $n$ (using lowercase $e \approx 2.718$ and lowercase exponent $n$, avoiding confusion with capital $\mathbb{E}$ for expectation or capital $N$ for normal distributions), stock prices can plunge 90% or 99%, but can never hit or cross below zero.

💡 Corporate Law Harmony: Stockholders cannot lose more than 100% of their investment, mathematically fulfilling limited liability.
3. Core Exam Takeaway Hull Exam Trap

The Itô Volatility Drag ($-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$)

⚠️ Expected Mean vs Typical Median:

While the theoretical mean stock price is $\mathbb{E}[S_T] = S_0 e^{\mu T}$, the median (typical path) grows at only $(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T$!

💡 Why Compounding Volatility Drags Down Wealth:

If you gain $+30\%$ then lose $-30\%$, you do not break even! You end at $1.30 \times 0.70 = 0.91$ (a $9\%$ net loss). Volatility drag is real and must be accounted for in options pricing.

🔗 Conceptual bridge: The stock price bridge
The flaw of dollar drift
Constant Dollar Moves Fail

If Berkshire Hathaway is $600,000 and Apple is $200, a $5 daily drift means 0.0008% vs 2.5%. Investors demand percentage returns!

The bridge: Multiply by $S$
$$dS = \mu S \, dt + \sigma S \, dz$$

Drift becomes $\mu S$, and volatility becomes $\sigma S$. Dividing by $S$ gives constant percentage returns!

What this unlocks
Protected Zero Floor ($S \gt 0$)

As price nears $0$, shocks shrink to $0$. Stock prices naturally obey limited liability and can never go negative.

The Exact Solution via Itô's Lemma

From differential percentage returns → $d(\ln S)$ → closed-form stock price $S(T)$.

Analytical Solution

In lectures, how do we solve $\frac{dS}{S} = \mu \, dt + \sigma \, dz$? We apply Itô's Lemma to the logarithmic transform $G(S) = \ln S$ first to obtain $dG = d(\ln S)$. Then, we exponentiate both sides to get the closed-form stock price $S(T)$:

Step 1: Log-price differential via Itô's Lemma ($dG$)
$$dG = d(\ln S) = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) dt + \sigma \, dz$$
Step 2: Exponentiated closed-form future stock price ($S(T)$)
$$S(T) = S_0 \exp\left( \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) T + \sigma \sqrt{T} Z \right), \quad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Notice the Itô correction term: $-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}$! It arises directly from the second derivative $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial S^2} = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$, keeping the expected value equal to $\mathbb{E}[S(T)] = S_0 e^{\mu T}$.

🔗 Step-by-step progression: $\frac{dS}{S} \;\to\; d(\ln S) \;\to\; S(T)$
Closed Form
Step 1: Percentage return SDE
Percentage Return SDE

Stock changes follow $\frac{dS}{S} = \mu \, dt + \sigma \, dz$.

Step 2: Log transform via Itô
Log Transform $G(S) = \ln S$

With $G' = \frac{1}{S}$ and $G'' = -\frac{1}{S^2}$, Itô yields $d\ln S = \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)dt + \sigma dz$.

Step 3: Exponentiation to $S(T)$
Exponentiated Safety ($S(T) \gt 0$)

Because $e^n \gt 0$ for any real exponent $n$, taking $\exp$ yields strictly positive prices ($S(T) \gt 0$), embedding limited liability!

📖

Textbook Breakdown: What is this formula & what is exp?

Directly from John C. Hull, Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives (Chapter 14, Sections 14.3 & 14.4).

Hull Ch. 14 Reference
1
Why does $\exp(\dots)$ appear?

$\exp(x)$ is standard notation for Euler's number $e$ raised to power $x$ ($e^x$, where $e \approx 2.71828$):

$$\exp(x) \equiv e^x$$

Why does it appear? In Itô's Lemma, we solved for log-price: $\ln\left(\frac{S(T)}{S_0}\right) = \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}Z$. To isolate $S(T)$, we must take the exponential function (anti-logarithm) on both sides: $S(T) = S_0 \cdot \exp(\dots)$!

2
Why lowercase $e^n \gt 0$ guarantees limited liability

Notation reminder: Always use lowercase $e \approx 2.718$ (Euler's constant) and lowercase exponent $n$ (any real power). Never confuse with capital $\mathbb{E}$ (Expectation) or capital $N$ (Normal distribution or path count).

Massive Bull Run ($n = +2$) $e^{+2} \approx 7.39 \gt 0$
Historic Crash ($n = -4$) $e^{-4} = \frac{1}{e^4} \approx 0.018 \gt 0$

Because $S_0 \gt 0$ and $e^n \gt 0$ for any real number $n$, their product $S(T) = S_0 e^n \gt 0$ stays strictly positive. Stock prices can never touch or cross below zero!

1. Formal Definition Discrete Algorithm

The Step-by-Step Simulation Formula (Hull Table 14.1)

$$\Delta S = \mu S \, \Delta t + \sigma S \, \epsilon \sqrt{\Delta t}$$

In theory, stocks follow continuous calculus: $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dz$. But computers cannot compute infinitesimals $dt$! Instead, John Hull converts it into small discrete steps $\Delta t$ (e.g. daily $\Delta t = \frac{1}{252}$ or weekly $\Delta t = \frac{1}{52}$). At each step, a standard normal draw $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ generates the next price:

$$S(t + \Delta t) = S(t)\exp\left(\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon\right)$$

Generating $N$ independent paths simulates future market trajectories, exactly reproducing John Hull Table 14.1.

2. In Plain English Core Intuition

Rolling the Market Dice for Parallel Universes

Nobody knows what surprise news tomorrow will bring. Monte Carlo rolls the dice across dozens of parallel financial universes:

🎲 Periodic Normal Draw ($\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$)

A random bell-curve draw at each step: positive for bullish news, negative for bearish news.

🧮 Compounded Step ($\Delta S$)

Stock price advances by expected period drift plus that period's shock jump ($\sigma S \epsilon \sqrt{\Delta t}$).

🌌 The Cloud of Futures

Viewing 100 paths together reveals the true probability distribution of gains, risk, and drawdowns.

💡 Quantitative Pricing: Instead of predicting one path, traders average discounted payoffs across simulated paths to price options.
3. Core Exam Takeaway Hull Exam Focus

Convergence Rate & Path Dependency

🎯 Law of Large Numbers Convergence:

As path count $N \to \infty$, the sample mean converges to theoretical $\mathbb{E}[S_T] = S_0 e^{\mu T}$. The standard error shrinks at rate $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}$.

💡 Step Choice: Daily (252) vs Weekly (52 / Hull Table 14.1):

John Hull Table 14.1 uses weekly steps ($\Delta t = 1/52 \approx 0.0192$), while daily stock simulation uses $\Delta t = 1/252$. Both converge to the identical continuous GBM as $\Delta t \to 0$!
